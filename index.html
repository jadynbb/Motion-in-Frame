How is confidence sold?

Gap shows what it feels like to have a place in a group and bring your own style to it. Kenzo shows what it feels like to decide how to act for yourself in a setting that expects you to conform.

What we can see

The overhead shots make that difference visible. In Kenzo, Qualley throws one arm diagonally across the carpet while the other bends close to her head. Her uneven reach and blurred hands give the movement the force of a release. In Gap, lines of dancers extend outward from KATSEYE. Their spacing and repeated arm positions create a pattern that depends on everyone moving together. Kenzo asks us to follow one person’s sudden burst of energy. Gap asks us to see what becomes possible when a whole group coordinates its movement.

Where confidence comes from

At Kenzo’s gala, Qualley feels the pressure to appear calm, graceful, and feminine on other people’s terms. Her unruly dancing makes it look exhilarating to let go of that pressure. When she leaps toward the floral eye, she’s ready to let others see the side of herself she once held back. Gap makes other people part of what gives its performers confidence. Moving in sync makes the dancers’ connection visible, and the way they arrange themselves can direct attention toward one performer. Gap makes standing out feel like something a community can make room for.

What we could take home

Grant McCracken explains how an ad can make us associate the feeling we get from a scene with the product it sells. Gap shows its denim on dancers who move together and help bring one another into the spotlight. The ad invites the viewer to imagine the confidence that comes from having a group behind her. The jeans offer a way to feel part of that community while wearing something she can style in her own way. Kenzo brings back the floral eye in the shape of the perfume bottle, linking the scent to Qualley’s decision to be seen on her own terms. The fragrance offers the imagined confidence to walk into a room with expectations of its own and decide how you want to show up. McCracken (1986)
